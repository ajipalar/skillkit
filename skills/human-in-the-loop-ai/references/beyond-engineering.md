# Beyond Engineering: AI for Writers, Researchers, and Creatives

AI-assisted work extends far beyond software engineering. The principles of effective
collaboration translate across domains, but each field has its own patterns, pitfalls,
and opportunities.

## Writing and Content Creation

### The first-draft machine model

AI is most effective as a first-draft generator or brainstorming partner. The
critical skill is knowing how to edit AI output rather than accepting it.

**Effective patterns:**

- **Provide voice and style samples.** Paste paragraphs of your own writing and say
  "write in this style." Without this, AI defaults to a generic, slightly corporate
  voice that sounds like no one in particular.

- **Use AI for structure, write the words yourself.** Use AI to outline, organize,
  and structure thoughts, then write the actual prose. The AI is a thinking partner,
  not a ghostwriter. This maintains your voice while leveraging AI for mechanics.

- **The expand/contract technique.** Write a rough version yourself, then ask AI
  to expand specific sections. Or write long, then ask AI to help cut. This keeps
  your voice while getting mechanical editing help.

- **Surprise as a creative tool.** Robin Sloan's approach: use AI to generate
  unexpected combinations that the human then curates. The skill is maintaining
  editorial judgment while remaining open to novelty.

### Writing anti-patterns

- Accepting AI-generated prose without rewriting in your own voice
- Using AI for the creative decisions (what to say) rather than the mechanical
  ones (how to organize, what to cut)
- Letting AI homogenize your style into the generic AI voice
- Using AI output as final draft rather than starting material

### Where AI genuinely helps writers

- Brainstorming and ideation (generating options, not making choices)
- Outlining and structure
- Research synthesis and summarization
- Mechanical editing (grammar, clarity, conciseness)
- Format conversion (blog post → thread, article → executive summary)
- Generating counterarguments to stress-test your thinking

## Research and Analysis

### AI as literature review accelerator

Researchers use AI to survey large bodies of work quickly, then dive deep into
the most relevant sources themselves. The skill: use AI for breadth, do depth
yourself.

### The steel man generator

Ask AI to generate the strongest counterarguments to your thesis. Genuinely useful
because AI can draw on a vast range of perspectives. The skill is engaging honestly
with the counterarguments rather than dismissing them.

### Multiple perspectives technique

Ask AI to analyze the same data from different theoretical frameworks. The human's
job is to synthesize and judge which perspective is most illuminating.

### Critical evaluation of citations

AI systems routinely hallucinate citations — inventing papers, authors, and journal
articles that don't exist. This is one of the most dangerous failure modes for
researchers.

Rule: ALWAYS verify that cited papers actually exist. Never trust an AI citation
without checking. Use PubMed, Google Scholar, or other databases to confirm.

### The rubber duck with knowledge

Explaining your analysis approach to AI is like rubber duck debugging — it clarifies
your own thinking. But with the added benefit that AI can spot logical errors or
suggest alternative approaches.

### Verification through redundancy

Ask AI to perform the same analysis in two different ways and compare results.
Discrepancies flag errors.

## Data Analysis

### AI as first-pass tool

Use AI for initial data cleaning, basic analysis, and chart generation. Apply domain
expertise to interpret results and catch statistical errors.

### Where AI helps analysts

- Data cleaning and transformation
- Exploratory analysis and pattern identification
- Code generation for statistical tests
- Visualization creation
- Report formatting and structuring

### Where AI struggles with data

- Causal inference (AI generates correlations, not causation)
- Domain-specific validity checks (AI doesn't know your field's standards)
- Statistical edge cases (AI may use wrong tests or make distributional assumptions)
- Interpreting results in context (what does this finding mean for the business?)

## Design and Visual Work

### Exploration, then selection

Generate many variations quickly, then apply design judgment to select and refine.
AI expands the design space; the designer navigates it.

### The mood board approach to prompting

Provide visual or textual examples of the desired aesthetic rather than trying to
describe it abstractly. This transfers the design practice of mood boarding into
AI interaction.

### Maggie Appleton's insight

The best prompts are essentially design documents — they specify constraints,
audiences, and success criteria. Design thinking (understanding users, defining
problems, prototyping solutions) applies directly to crafting effective AI
interactions.

## Education and Learning

### Mollick's 70% framework

When using AI to learn, ask it to solve 70% of the problem and leave the remaining
30% for you. This keeps you cognitively engaged while still getting productivity
benefits.

### Tutor vs coder

AI can be a tutor that explains concepts and helps you understand, or a coder that
does the work for you. Use it as a tutor when learning, as a coder when producing.
Most people default to coder mode and miss the learning opportunity.

### The Socratic pattern

Instead of asking AI for answers, ask it to ask you questions:
"I'm learning about X. Ask me questions to test my understanding. Start easy and
get harder. Explain when I'm wrong."

### Teaching AI to teach you

- "Explain this like I'm [level]"
- "What's the intuition behind this?"
- "What would I need to understand to come up with this solution myself?"
- "What's the simplest version of this concept?"

## Project Management and Non-Technical Work

### AI for task decomposition

Non-engineers can use AI to break large projects into actionable tasks. The pattern:
describe the project and constraints, ask AI to create a structured task breakdown.

### Meeting and communication assistance

- Summarizing meeting notes into action items
- Drafting communications in appropriate tone
- Preparing briefing documents from raw data
- Translating technical concepts for non-technical audiences

### Process documentation

AI is effective at converting informal knowledge ("how do we do X") into structured
process documentation. The human provides the knowledge, AI provides the structure.

## Universal Principles That Transfer

These patterns work regardless of domain:

### 1. Verify AI's factual claims

AI confidently states things that aren't true — across all domains, not just code.
Any factual claim should be verified, especially citations, statistics, dates, and
specific technical details.

### 2. AI for options, human for choices

AI is excellent at generating options, alternatives, and possibilities. It is poor
at choosing between them. Use AI to expand your option space, then apply your own
judgment.

### 3. Specificity of input determines quality of output

This is universal. Vague requests get generic responses. Specific requests with
examples, constraints, and context get useful responses. Show, don't just tell.

### 4. The first draft is never the final draft

Treat all AI output as starting material, not finished product. This applies to
code, prose, analysis, designs, and plans.

### 5. Maintain your own expertise

Use AI to amplify your existing skills, not replace them. If you stop practicing
the underlying skill, you lose the ability to judge AI output quality.

### 6. Fresh contexts for fresh problems

The principle of clearing context between tasks applies to all domains. A
conversation that accumulated discussion about topic A will produce worse results
on topic B.

### 7. Break big tasks into small pieces

Large, vague requests fail across all domains. Small, specific requests with
clear success criteria succeed. This is the most reliably transferable pattern.

## The Non-Engineer Advantage

Non-engineers sometimes have advantages in AI collaboration:

- **Less tempted by technical shortcuts.** They focus on outcomes rather than
  implementation elegance.
- **More comfortable specifying in natural language.** They don't try to write
  pseudo-code or use technical jargon when plain language would be clearer.
- **Lower investment in "doing it themselves."** They're more willing to delegate
  and iterate.
- **Better at describing what "good" looks like.** Domain expertise translates
  directly into effective AI prompting when you know what you want.

The main disadvantage: harder to verify technical output. This makes the
verification strategies even more important — find ways to check that AI output
is correct that don't require deep technical knowledge (run the code, compare
outputs, test with known examples).
